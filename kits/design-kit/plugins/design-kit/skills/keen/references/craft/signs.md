# Signs

Every visual choice means something before anyone reads it. Design with what it means, not only with how it looks.

## Moves

- **Know what kind of sign you are using.** Charles Sanders Peirce separated three kinds: an icon resembles its object (a drawn envelope for mail), an index is caused by or points to it (a footprint, a progress bar filling), a symbol means by agreement only (a hamburger for a menu, red for stop). Icons travel well; symbols must be learned; indexes feel truthful because they are connected to what they show. A product built on indexes (a chart that is the real data, a mark that is the real state) earns trust that decoration cannot.
- **Separate what it shows from what it suggests.** Roland Barthes ("Rhetoric of the Image", 1964) separated denotation (what is depicted) from connotation (what it implies). A serif face denotes letters and connotes tradition; a gradient denotes colour and connotes the technology industry of a particular decade. Ask what each choice connotes and whether that is what the product means.
- **Borrow a sign system, not a style.** The richest material is the visual language of the product's own world: nautical charts, railway timetables, lab notebooks, field guides, ledgers, sheet music. Their conventions were refined by use, and borrowing their structure (a chart's soundings, a timetable's grid, a ledger's ruled column) brings meaning with it.
- **Icons as a system.** Otl Aicher's pictograms for Munich 1972 work because they share one grid, one stroke and one set of angles. Icons drawn to one rule read as a language; icons collected from several sets read as clip art. Prefer one set, at one stroke weight, at sizes that match the type. Pair icons with words wherever space allows: an icon alone is a symbol, and symbols must be learned.
- **Emoji are not icons.** They render differently on every platform, carry tone of voice, and say "unfinished" in product interfaces. `fingerprint.mjs` flags them.
- **Conventions are invariants for controls.** A link looks like a link, a button like a button, a toggle like a toggle. Reinvent the surface around controls freely; reinventing what a control looks like costs every user a moment of doubt.
- **The mark and the name are commitments.** Treat the logo, product name and its main colour as approved unless the person says otherwise.

## Checks

- For each strong visual element, write what it connotes in three words. Are those words the product's?
- Could a person who has never seen the product guess what it is about from the first screen with all words blurred?

## Sources
